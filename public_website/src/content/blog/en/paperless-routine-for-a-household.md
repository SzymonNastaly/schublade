Most attempts to go paperless at home fail for the same reason. They start with a big scanning weekend and a folder structure, and three months later the folders are out of date and the paper is back on the kitchen table. A routine that lasts is small. It asks for a few minutes a week and makes no decisions you can put off.

This post describes such a routine. It works with any document archive, and it notes where Schublade takes a step off your hands.

## One place where paper lands

Pick one tray, one box, or one corner of a shelf. Every letter that needs keeping goes there, unopened or opened, and nowhere else. Not the kitchen counter, not the hallway, not the car.

The point of the tray is that you never have to search the house. If a letter is not in the tray and not in the archive, it is still in the post box.

Open the post the day it arrives and throw out the advertising right away. Everything else goes into the tray. Do not sort it there.

## A fixed time to empty the tray

Choose one slot a week, the same day and time. Fifteen minutes on Sunday evening is enough for most households. In that slot you scan everything in the tray and decide what happens to the paper afterwards.

A weekly slot beats scanning each letter the moment it arrives. You do not need a scanner set up all the time, and a missed day does not leave a gap. If a letter needs action sooner, such as a bill with a short due date, handle the action when it arrives and scan it in the slot.

## Scan with the phone

A flatbed scanner is no longer needed for household paper. A phone camera reads a letter well enough, if you give it good conditions.

- Lay the page flat on a dark, plain surface, so the edges stand out.
- Use daylight or an even lamp, and avoid the shadow of your own hand and phone.
- Fit the whole page in the frame, and hold the phone parallel to the paper.
- Keep pages of one letter together as one document.

A document scanner app finds the page edges, straightens the perspective, and saves all pages as one PDF. With Schublade, the [iPhone app](/iphone/) does this with the system's document camera and sends the PDF straight to your archive. Without an iPhone, the [web app](/web/) works from the phone's home screen and takes photos with the camera.

You do not need to worry about which way up the phone was. Schublade applies the photo's rotation tag, and it reads a sideways page at all four rotations when the first reading fails. ## Let email go straight to the archive

More and more paperwork never exists on paper. Invoices, contracts, and statements arrive as email attachments or as downloads from a customer portal.

Give your archive an email address and forward those messages to it. With Schublade, you set up the address when you deploy it and name the people allowed to send from their own addresses. Schublade archives the attachments. Schublade prints a message without an attachment, such as an order confirmation, to a PDF and archives that.

Downloads from portals land in a folder on your computer. Upload them in the slot, or let a program collect them. The [Mac app](/mac/), coming soon, watches folders such as Downloads and sends only files the archive does not hold yet.

## Name nothing by hand

The most expensive habit in a paperless setup is naming files. `2026-10-14_Stadtwerke_Strom_Abschlag.pdf` takes a minute to type, and the scheme drifts after a few months.

Skip it. A good archive reads the document and names it for you. Schublade gives each document a title, a date, a sender, a category, and the household member it concerns, and it extracts fields such as amounts and IBANs. You find documents by searching for a word you remember, or by filtering by sender or category. No folders to maintain means no folders to get wrong.

If the archive gets a detail wrong, correct it when you notice. Do not review every document in advance.

## Check what failed

Once a week, in the same slot, look at the documents the archive could not process. In Schublade they appear under Failed documents, with a sentence that says what went wrong. Retry the ones that failed for a passing reason. Delete the ones that are unreadable, and scan the paper again if you still have it.

## Decide what to keep on paper

A scan is a good copy, and for many letters it is all you need. Some documents are different. Certificates, notarized contracts, official documents with a seal, and anything you may have to present as an original are worth keeping on paper.

How long you must keep which document, and whether a scan may replace the paper, depends on your country, your situation, and the kind of document. Tax records and records of a business have their own rules. Check what applies to you with your tax authority or an adviser before you shred anything.

For the paper you keep, one box per year is enough. You do not need to sort it, because the archive is your index. If you ever need an original, the scan's date tells you which box to open.

## Share it with the household

A household archive works best when everyone can add to it and find things in it. Schublade serves one household per deployment, and everyone who can sign in sees every document. Agree who empties the tray, and let everyone forward their own email.

## The routine, in short

1. Open post the day it arrives. Advertising goes out, everything else into the tray.
2. Forward email invoices and statements to the archive.
3. Once a week, scan the tray with the phone.
4. Check failed documents in the same slot.
5. Put paper originals you keep into this year's box.
6. Name nothing, sort nothing. Search.
